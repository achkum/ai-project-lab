ST2 — Make guitar practice feedback less likely to jump to the wrong octave
Investigated 2026-11-02 | KEEP | kept_as_investigated_possibility

A practice tool can tell a guitarist that a correct note is an octave too high when the microphone or pickup emphasises harmonics. A compact adapted model could make this feedback more dependable.
Example: The same single-note phrase is recorded through a pickup and a room microphone. The displayed notes should remain consistent across both recordings.

What the study would try
Adapt a small pitch model using paired microphone and pickup recordings, and compare it with established pitch trackers and smoothing.
Everyday question: Can the same guitar phrase produce more consistent notes across recording channels?
Expected research output: A small pitch-correction model, an audited evaluation set and a report on accuracy and feedback delay.

What was actually checked
Read primary methods, licenses and errata; bounded annotation/audio access checks only.
Read a WAV prefix and a complete 34.90-second solo JAMS: 78 notes, six pitch-contour/note tracks, and 24.26 seconds with exactly one active annotated note.
Model experiments completed: 0. Runtime targets below are planned limits, not measured results.

Qualification and research depth
Kept as an investigated possibility because the scope combines explicit model adaptation with a justified controlled comparison, grouped evaluation and measurable failure trade-offs. Data access and primary terms were inspected. This is a feasibility judgment, not completed model evidence, a novelty claim or LTU approval.
The project adapts a pretrained acoustic model, audits real timing annotations, uses a performer-held-out design, compares neural adaptation with a strong classical pitch tracker and structured smoothing, and analyses accuracy versus latency. It is not a full music-transcription app.

Scientific question and hypothesis
Can a small temporal correction model trained on frozen pitch-network activations improve monophonic guitar pitch and octave consistency across performers and recording channels?
A learned temporal correction head will reduce octave errors under channel changes beyond frame smoothing alone, without hiding genuine pitch changes or increasing voiced/unvoiced mistakes.

Data access and rights
GuitarSet v1.1.0
Public Zenodo ZIPs support selective range reads. Select solo tracks and annotated intervals with exactly one active note; do not assume all guitar audio is monophonic.
360 paired recording/annotation entries. Annotation ZIP 39.1 MB; mono microphone 656.9 MB; pickup mix 683.1 MB. Start with at most 20–30 minutes of eligible monophonic material across six performer IDs.
Terms: CC-BY-4.0 dataset. Preserve citation/attribution and an explicit annotation-correction/exclusion manifest.
Still to verify: Count usable time per performer/channel, ensure paired alignment, audit rests and note overlap. Known comp-track offset and duplicate-note issues are documented; solos-only scope excludes those named tracks but is not a blanket label-quality guarantee.

Model adaptation
CREPE tiny pretrained pitch network; freeze it and cache its activation vectors.
Train a small temporal convolution or linear temporal correction head over short activation sequences, with an explicit voiced/unvoiced output. Compare a learned transition prior with fixed Viterbi/HMM smoothing.
Keep the added head below 100,000 parameters and the temporal window short enough to report causal and delayed variants.
Model terms: CREPE project MIT. Pin the official tiny-weight release and its integrity/terms before any future weight download.

Comparators
- YIN or pYIN, with their normal voicing/temporal settings.
- Unmodified CREPE tiny and its existing Viterbi smoothing.
- A simple median filter and a calibration-only head on identical activations.

Evaluation and leakage controls
Leave one performer out; put all channels, derived windows and altered versions of a recording in the same fold. Use separate development performers for settings. Evaluate microphone-to-pickup and pickup-to-microphone transfer. Synthetic noise/equalisation are optional labelled stress conditions, never the only test.

Ablations
- Temporal correction versus per-frame calibration.
- Channel augmentation versus real paired-channel training.
- Voicing loss and transition smoothing; causal versus look-ahead context.

Measurements
Raw pitch accuracy within a specified cents tolerance, raw chroma accuracy, octave-error rate, voicing precision/recall, note-onset F1 where labels support it, processing time and algorithmic delay. Show every held-out performer; uncertainty with only six performers will be wide.

CPU plan and cheaper fallback
Cache at most a few hundred thousand 360-bin activation vectors (hundreds of MB). Tiny-network extraction is the main CPU cost; set a pilot-based cap of one overnight extraction and at most two hours per head experiment. Peak target 4 GB; no large music model training.
Learn a small classifier choosing between CREPE and pYIN proposals using their confidence and local signal features. Preserve the same held-out protocol.

Closest work and proposed contribution
CREPE already supports temporal smoothing, and October SheetSage2 uses structured decoding for coherent transcription. This study isolates a much narrower paired-channel guitar adaptation problem; it does not claim invention of temporal consistency or reproduce the larger transcription system.

Risks and kill test
A strong pYIN baseline may already solve clean single-note portions. Annotation errors can masquerade as model errors. Selecting only easy monophonic fragments may reduce practical coverage, which must be reported. A performer holdout does not by itself establish generalisation to unseen musical compositions.
Verify at least several independent solo recordings and usable single-note time for every performer. Check 20 hand-inspected intervals against labels and paired waveforms. Withdraw the octave-specific claim if errors are overwhelmingly annotation offsets or if the strong baseline leaves no measurable headroom.
